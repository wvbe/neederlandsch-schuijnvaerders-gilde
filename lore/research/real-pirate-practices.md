# Real pirate practice, 1600–1725: a sourced dossier

This dossier is the historical bedrock under the Neederlandsch Schuijnvaerders Gilde (NSG, trading in English ports as Pirate Shipping Inc): a Dutch N.V. seated in Vlissingen, founded 1621, with five chambers at Vlissingen, Baltimore (West Cork), Port Royal, Île Sainte-Marie and Ambon in the reported year and a sixth at Vava'u, Tonga, opened in 2025 (invented), 25 wooden ships, about 2,300 people, and a founding rule that permits only technology in use aboard a Dutch ship in the 1600s. It records what 17th- and early 18th-century pirates, privateers and Dutch trading companies actually did, with sources, and states for each practice how the fictional company adopts it and where it bends the record. It is the only file in this repository written in plain expert English rather than the company voice, because its job is to be checkable. The rule for every other file is simple: nothing in the lore may contradict this record unless the departure is explicitly tagged "invented". Claims this dossier could not verify are tagged "unverified" or "invented" in the closing section.

## Practices

### 1. Articles of agreement and ship's codes

**Record.** Pirate crews of the Golden Age sailed under written articles that every man signed or swore to. Only a handful survive, all via Captain Charles Johnson's *A General History of the Pyrates* (1724), chiefly those of Bartholomew Roberts (eleven articles, c. 1721) and John Phillips (nine articles, sworn aboard the *Revenge* on 29 August 1723). Roberts' articles gave every man a vote and an equal claim on provisions, banned gambling, put lights out at eight, forbade women and boys aboard on pain of death, and punished desertion in battle with death or marooning. Phillips' code ordered "Moses' Law" (thirty-nine lashes) for striking a shipmate, punished smoking in the hold or carrying an unshielded candle, and stripped the share of any man who neglected his arms. The older buccaneer equivalent was the *chasse-partie* described by Alexandre Exquemelin, a barber-surgeon who sailed with Henry Morgan and published *De Americaensche Zee-Roovers* in Amsterdam in 1678 (English 1684): shares, fixed payments to surgeon and carpenter, and compensation for wounds, all agreed before any prize was taken.

**Sources.** [Pirate code (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirate_code); [Roberts' articles, 1721](https://piratedocuments.com/bartholomew-roberts-articles-1721/); [Johnson, ch. XV, John Phillips](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/captain-charles-johnson/a-general-history-of-the-pirates/text/chapter-17); [Alexandre Exquemelin (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Exquemelin).

**NSG uses it.** The company's constitution is called the Articles of Agreement and is signed by every crew member on enrolment. Its structure follows Roberts and Phillips: votes, shares, safety rules (lights, powder, smoking), a duty to keep arms and tools serviceable, and a compensation schedule. The Articles double as the code of conduct tested under ESRS G1.

**NSG changes it.** The death penalties, corporal punishment and the exclusion of women are removed. The historical ban on "boys" is inverted into an adults-only rule with a stated minimum age, and the article punishing violence against women becomes a general anti-harassment clause. Roberts' curfew survives as a rest-hours rule.

### 2. The share system and "no prey, no pay"

**Record.** Buccaneers and pirates were not waged; they were paid in shares of what they took, on the principle Exquemelin renders as "no prey, no pay" (in English editions also "no purchase, no pay"). Roberts' articles gave the captain and quartermaster two shares each, the master, boatswain and gunner one and a half, other officers one and a quarter, and every other man one. Phillips' crew gave the captain one and a half and the master, carpenter, boatswain and gunner one and a quarter. Exquemelin's *chasse-partie* set aside fixed sums off the top before shares: about 200 pieces of eight for provisions, 100 to 150 for the carpenter, and 200 to 250 for the surgeon and his medicine chest.

**Sources.** [Pirate code (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirate_code); [Governance in 18th-century piracy (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governance_in_18th-century_piracy); [The Code of the Brethren of the Coast](https://waynesavage.com/the-code-of-the-brethren-of-the-coast/).

**NSG uses it.** Pay is a base wage plus a share of voyage results on the historical ladder (2 : 1.5 : 1.25 : 1); provisions, surgeon's chest and carpenter's stores are charged off the top. The crew's minority "parten" interest in each hull is the accounting bridge between share pay and a single reportable undertaking.

**NSG changes it.** "No prey, no pay" is softened: a guaranteed base wage exists, because a wholly variable wage would be a reportable S1 negative impact (irregular pay) that the company only partly remediates. That irregularity is disclosed, not hidden.

### 3. Injury compensation schedules

**Record.** Exquemelin gives the buccaneers' tariff: 600 pieces of eight (or six enslaved people) for the loss of a right arm, 500 for a left arm, 500 for a right leg, 400 for a left leg, 100 for an eye, and 100 for a finger. Roberts' articles paid 800 dollars (pieces of eight) for a lost limb and proportionally for lesser hurts; Phillips' paid 400 for a joint and 800 for a limb. Payments came out of the common stock before shares were divided. The alternative payment "in slaves" shows that the buccaneer economy priced enslaved people as a unit of value.

**Sources.** [Pirate Surgeon's Journal, surgeon history p. 4](https://piratesurgeon.com/pages/surgeon_pages/surgeon_history4.htm); [Pirate code (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirate_code); [Johnson, ch. XV](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/captain-charles-johnson/a-general-history-of-the-pirates/text/chapter-17).

**NSG uses it.** The Articles carry a fixed compensation schedule paid from the common chest before any distribution, right-left asymmetry included. It underpins the S1 disclosures on work-related injuries and their remediation.

**NSG changes it.** Amounts are restated in EUR in any figure that reaches a metric; the payment "in slaves" is struck out entirely and recorded in the code of conduct as the historical wrong the company repudiates.

### 4. Elected captains and the quartermaster's counterweight

**Record.** Pirate captains were elected by the crew and could be deposed by it; their authority was absolute only in chase and battle. The quartermaster was elected separately as the crew's trustee: he divided plunder, kept the common stock, settled quarrels, administered punishments the company voted, and often commanded a captured prize. The whole company sitting as a council decided where to sail and whether to fight. Buccaneer fleets such as Morgan's Panama expedition of 1670 to 1671 were governed by a council of captains under written articles.

**Sources.** [Governance in 18th-century piracy (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governance_in_18th-century_piracy); [Pirate code (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirate_code).

**NSG uses it.** Ships' councils elect captains; captains form the Council of Captains under an elected Captain-General; a Quartermaster-General elected by all crews keeps the common chest and holds a veto on distributions. This is the management tier of the two-tier board described in the decisions file, with the quartermaster as the natural whistleblowing and sustainability officer.

**NSG changes it.** Deposing a captain by acclamation is replaced with a documented vote and a supervisory tier (the Heeren Vijf) that never existed on a pirate ship, borrowed instead from the VOC's chamber directors.

### 5. Marooning and discipline

**Record.** The characteristic pirate punishment was marooning: putting a man ashore on an uninhabited cay with, in Phillips' words, "one bottle of powder, one bottle of water, one small arm, and shot". Phillips prescribed it for running away or keeping secrets from the company, and marooning or shooting for theft or gambling above a piece of eight. Roberts' crew marooned any man who defrauded the company of a dollar's worth, and slit the ears and nose of a repeat offender before setting him ashore. Flogging ("Moses' Law") was used, though less than in the Royal Navy.

**Sources.** [Johnson, ch. XV](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/captain-charles-johnson/a-general-history-of-the-pirates/text/chapter-17); [Bartholomew Roberts (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartholomew_Roberts).

**NSG uses it.** "Marooning" survives as the word for the company's final disciplinary sanction: removal from ship and share and transfer ashore, with the bottle of water and the small arm reduced to ceremonial symbols handed over at the hearing.

**NSG changes it.** No corporal punishment, no mutilation, no abandonment. Discipline is a written procedure: hearing before the quartermaster, appeal to the Council of Captains.

### 6. Careening and ship maintenance

**Record.** Wooden hulls in tropical water were attacked by the shipworm *Teredo navalis* and fouled by weed and barnacles that could cost up to three knots of speed. Pirates careened every two or three months: the ship was heeled on a beach, the hull "breamed" by burning off growth with torches of dried grass, worm-eaten planks replaced, and the bottom coated with tar, tallow and sulphur. Seventeenth-century mariners also tried lead sheathing, which corroded the iron fastenings; copper sheathing came only in 1761 with HMS *Alarm*. A careened ship was defenceless, so careening grounds were chosen for concealment.

**Sources.** [Careening (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Careening); [Pirate Ships of the West (Vallar)](http://www.cindyvallar.com/PirateShipsWest.html); [Copper sheathing (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_sheathing).

**NSG uses it.** Every ship careens quarterly at its chamber; October, closing the Kermis year, is the fleet-wide careening month. Tar, tallow and sulphur consumed there give E2 (pollution) its most concrete data.

**NSG changes it.** Nothing material; the company simply records what the record leaves silent (quantities of tar and tallow, disposal of burnt-off growth). Copper sheathing is not used, because it postdates the technology cut-off.

### 7. Provisioning: hardtack, salt meat, drink and scurvy

**Record.** The staples were ship's biscuit (hardtack, *scheepsbeschuit*), salted beef and pork, dried fish, peas, groats and beer or wine, with water that spoiled fast. The buccaneers' name comes from the *boucan*, the frame on which Hispaniola's hunters smoked meat. Scurvy killed more sailors than battle or shipwreck between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries; the VOC founded its Cape refreshment station in 1652 partly to supply passing fleets with fresh food. The cure was not established until James Lind's controlled trial of 1747, and the Royal Navy only issued lemon juice generally from 1795. "Grog" is later still: Admiral Vernon ordered rum diluted with water on 21 August 1740, and the word is his nickname. Seventeenth-century crews drank beer, wine, brandy and, in the Caribbean after about 1650, rum.

**Sources.** [Hardtack (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardtack); [Grog (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grog); [Lind discovers a cure for scurvy (EBSCO)](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/lind-discovers-cure-scurvy); [Buccaneer (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buccaneer).

**NSG uses it.** Ration tables in biscuit, salt meat, peas and beer; a victualling clerk per chamber; food and drink feed S1 (health) and E5 (resource use).

**NSG changes it.** The company uses citrus as folk practice, but the lore must not call the drink "grog" or describe a lime ration as formal policy; "grog" goes on the anachronism list.

### 8. Navigation of the period

**Record.** Latitude came from the cross-staff or the backstaff (Davis quadrant, described in John Davis's *Seaman's Secrets*, 1594), read against printed declination tables. Speed came from the log line and sandglass, described in William Bourne's *A Regiment for the Sea* (1574, Dutch editions from 1594). Depth and bottom came from the lead line. Position between fixes was dead reckoning on a traverse board, plotted on portolan, plane or Mercator charts. The Dutch dominated the chart trade: Lucas Janszoon Waghenaer's *Spieghel der Zeevaerdt* (1584) was the first printed pilot atlas, Hessel Gerritsz became the VOC's first official cartographer in October 1617, Willem Blaeu succeeded him, and Johannes van Keulen's *Zee-Fakkel* (from 1681) dominated the next century. VOC charts were company secrets. Longitude at sea was not measurable: Hadley's octant dates to 1731, the sextant to 1757, and Harrison's H4 was first tried at sea in 1761.

**Sources.** [Backstaff (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backstaff); [William Bourne (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bourne_(mathematician)); [Lucas Janszoon Waghenaer (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas_Janszoon_Waghenaer); [Hessel Gerritsz (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hessel_Gerritsz); [Harrison's clocks (Royal Museums Greenwich)](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem).

**NSG uses it.** Navigators carry backstaff, cross-staff, log line, lead line, sandglasses and manuscript charts; a chart room at Middelburg treats them as trade secrets in the VOC manner.

**NSG changes it.** Nothing. The absence of longitude is a real operating constraint and a genuine safety risk the company reports under S1 and disaster-risk disclosures.

### 9. Gunnery and small arms

**Record.** Ships of the period carried muzzle-loading smooth-bore guns of bronze or cast iron on wooden truck carriages, classed by shot weight and name (cannon, demi-cannon, culverin, demi-culverin, saker, minion) and served with linstock and slow match. Shot was solid iron, or grape and chain to cut rigging and men. Small arms were matchlock and, increasingly, flintlock muskets and pistols, with cutlass, boarding axe and pike for close work; hand grenades (grenadoes) were in use. Pirates preferred intimidation to gunnery, because a shattered prize was worth less; Roberts, killed by grapeshot at Cape Lopez on 10 February 1722, was the exception. Navies had boys (powder monkeys) carry cartridges from the magazine.

**Sources.** [Naval artillery in the Age of Sail (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_artillery_in_the_Age_of_Sail); [Battle of Cape Lopez (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cape_Lopez).

**NSG uses it.** Guns are carried and fired only as warning shots and salutes; gunpowder is the largest hazardous material in E2 and the gunner a named safety role.

**NSG changes it.** No lethal violence in the reporting period: guns are not fired into ships. Powder is handled by adults, never boys. The company's rules of engagement (stop, board, inspect papers) are documented under G1.

### 10. Letters of marque, bonds, prize courts and ransom bills

**Record.** A privateer was a private ship licensed by a state to take enemy shipping under a letter of marque; without one, the same act was piracy. In 1597 the States General settled naval administration on five Admiralty colleges (Rotterdam, Amsterdam, Zeeland at Middelburg, the Noorderkwartier at Hoorn and Enkhuizen, and Friesland), each with jurisdiction over prizes. A commissievaarder needed a commission issued in the Stadholder's name, and the owners posted a bond: 20,000 guilders during the Second and Third Anglo-Dutch Wars, raised to 30,000 guilders in 1705. A prize's papers went to the Admiralty, which ruled whether it was "good prize"; released ships cost the privateer's sponsors the court costs; the *vendumeester* auctioned condemned goods. The Stadholder as Admiral-General took a tenth of net prize proceeds, about 700,000 guilders in the case of the 1628 silver fleet. England ran the same machinery through the High Court of Admiralty: bonds of £1,500 for a ship under 150 men and £3,000 above, standing interrogatories for captured crews, a ban on "breaking bulk" before sentence, and the Crown or Lord High Admiral retaining a tenth to a fifth. The surviving Prize Papers (documents and interrogatories seized with each prize) are a major archive of the period. Ransoming a captured ship against a written ransom bill was legal until Britain banned it in 1782 (repealed 1864). Jamaica's governor Modyford commissioned Morgan's buccaneers against Spain until the Treaty of Madrid (1670) ended the practice.

**Sources.** [Dutch admiralties (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_admiralties); [Wat is kaapvaart? (Zeeuwse Ankers)](https://www.zeeuwseankers.nl/verhaal/wat-is-kaapvaart); [Kaapvaart: expeditie en vendu (Zeeuwse Ankers)](https://www.zeeuwseankers.nl/verhaal/kaapvaart-expeditie-en-vendu); [Frederik Hendrik (nl.wikipedia)](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederik_Hendrik_van_Oranje); [Letter of marque (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_of_marque); [Licence to plunder (Legion Magazine)](https://legionmagazine.com/licence-to-plunder/); [Ransom (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ransom); [Treaty of Madrid, 1670 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Madrid_(1670)).

**NSG uses it.** The interdiction service follows this procedure: a client's commission is registered at Middelburg with a bond; papers and crew are examined under a written interrogatory; cargo is sealed and brought in without breaking bulk; an internal Prize Council rules "good prize" or orders restitution with costs to the client; sale is by auction or release against a ransom bill; a chamber fee stands in for the Admiralty share. The per-seizure file is the company's evidence archive.

**NSG changes it.** Clients, not states, issue the commissions (invented); the refusal conditions (no EU or allied vessels, no truce-breaking, no pressing of captured crews, no hostage-taking) are stricter than any historical court. Bond sizes and the chamber fee percentage are set in canon, not taken from the record (see Unverified).

### 11. The Dutch context: VOC, WIC, chambers, partenrederij and the fluyt

**Record.** The VOC (chartered 1602) was governed by the Heeren XVII: eight directors from Amsterdam, four from Zeeland, one each from Delft, Rotterdam, Hoorn and Enkhuizen, and a seventeenth seat rotating among the smaller chambers to check Amsterdam. The WIC (chartered 3 June 1621) had five chambers (Amsterdam, Zeeland, the Maze, the Noorderkwartier, Stad en Lande) and a board of nineteen. Both companies owned their ships outright; ordinary Dutch shipping ran on the *partenrederij*, a ship divided into shares of 1/8 to 1/64 held by merchants, skipper and shipwright and managed by a *boekhouder*. The fluyt, developed at Hoorn around 1595, was a pear-hulled, lightly armed bulk carrier sailed by a fraction of the crew of a comparable English ship.

**Sources.** [Heeren XVII (Encyclopedia.com)](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/heeren-xvii); [Dutch West India Company (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_West_India_Company); [Fluyt (Deutsches Historisches Museum)](https://www.dhm.de/mediathek/en/ship-types/milestones-in-the-history-of-european-shipbuilding/09-fluyt/).

**NSG uses it.** Five chambers (a sixth, Vava'u, opened in 2025, is invented and has no 1600s precedent), a rotating presidency, company-owned hulls assigned to chambers, a *boekhouder* who becomes the Advocaat-Boekhouder, and eight fluyts in the fleet. The name "chamber" for a base, and the seat at Vlissingen with the audit chamber at Middelburg, are direct borrowings.

**NSG changes it.** The Heeren XVII's seventeen seats are cut to five (the Heeren Vijf) and paired with an elected management board that no chartered company had (invented). The crews' minority *parten* interest in each hull blends the two ownership models that were in fact alternatives.

### 12. Piet Heyn's silver fleet (1628) and Claes Compaen

**Record.** On 8 September 1628 the WIC fleet under Piet Heyn trapped the New Spain treasure fleet in Matanzas Bay, Cuba, and took silver, gold and goods valued at 11,509,524 guilders. The WIC paid a 50 per cent dividend for the year; Heyn himself received 7,000 guilders, the Stadholder his tenth (about 700,000), and the crews' complaints about their share soured Heyn's relations with the company. Claes Gerritszoon Compaen of Oostzaan sailed as a privateer, turned pirate in 1623, took hundreds of ships from Salé in Morocco, petitioned the States General in 1626 and received a conditional pardon in 1627; he retired to Oostzaan.

**Sources.** [Battle in the Bay of Matanzas (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_in_the_Bay_of_Matanzas); [Piet Heyn and the Silver Fleet (History Today)](https://www.historytoday.com/archive/piet-heyn-and-silver-fleet); [Claes Gerritszoon Compaen (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claes_Gerritszoon_Compaen).

**NSG uses it.** Matanzas is the company's "founding prize" and the origin of its share-out pattern; Compaen is the cautionary tale in the code of conduct about what happens when a licensed raider drops the licence.

**NSG changes it.** NSG's participation in or windfall from the 1628 fleet is invented; the real prize belonged to the WIC. The lore should say the company "sailed in the shadow of" Heyn, not with him.

### 13. Caribbean havens: Tortuga, Port Royal, Nassau

**Record.** French hunters settled northern Hispaniola from about 1625 and moved to Tortuga around 1630; in 1640 Jean Le Vasseur fortified it with the forty-gun Fort de Rocher. Port Royal, Jamaica, was the English buccaneer capital from 1655; its governors issued anti-Spanish commissions until 1670. On 7 June 1692 an earthquake and tsunami sank about thirty-three acres of the town into the harbour, killing about 2,000 people at once and perhaps 3,000 more from injury and disease. Nassau hosted the loose "Republic of Pirates" from about 1706 until Woodes Rogers arrived as governor in July 1718 with a pardon and the Royal Navy.

**Sources.** [Fort de Rocher (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_de_Rocher); [1692 Jamaica earthquake (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1692_Jamaica_earthquake); [Woodes Rogers (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodes_Rogers); [Republic of Pirates (EBSCO)](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/republic-pirates).

**NSG uses it.** Port Royal is the Caribbean chamber; the 1692 earthquake is the base's "rebuilt on the spit" story and the anchor for its disaster-risk disclosure. Tortuga and Nassau appear as historical trading partners and careening grounds.

**NSG changes it.** A Dutch company holding a chamber in English Port Royal from the 1650s is invented; it is explained in the backstory as a tenancy under Modyford's commissions.

### 14. The Indian Ocean pirate round: Sainte-Marie, Every, Angre

**Record.** From Thomas Tew's voyage of 1693, Atlantic pirates sailed the "Pirate Round" round the Cape to prey on Mughal and East India Company shipping, wintering at Madagascar. Adam Baldridge set up a fortified trading post on Île Sainte-Marie about 1690 to 1691, supplied from New York by the merchant Frederick Philipse, selling stores and drink to pirates and dealing in enslaved Malagasy. In 1697 the islanders rose and killed around thirty pirates after Baldridge sold a group of locals into slavery; Baldridge fled and the Round declined. On 7 September 1695 Henry Every took the Mughal pilgrim ship *Ganj-i-Sawai*, with up to £600,000 in coin and jewels; the atrocities aboard led Aurangzeb to close ports to the English and forced the East India Company to convoy Indian shipping. Kanhoji Angre commanded the Maratha navy as *Sarkhel* from 1698 to 1729, selling passes to European shipping and never defeated at sea. Johnson's "Libertalia" is fiction. Later Asian piracy (the Iranun and Balangingi of the Sulu Sea, 1770s to 1840s; Zheng Yi Sao, 1807 to 1810) lies outside the period and rests on slave raiding.

**Sources.** [Pirate Round (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirate_Round); [Adam Baldridge (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Baldridge); [Sainte-Marie pirate massacre, 1697 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sainte-Marie_pirate_massacre_(1697)); [Ganj-i-Sawai (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganj-i-Sawai); [Kanhoji Angre (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanhoji_Angre); [Libertalia (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertalia).

**NSG uses it.** Île Sainte-Marie is the Indian Ocean chamber and victualling point; the 1697 uprising is the stated origin of the "no forced labour, no slave dealing" clause; the *Ganj-i-Sawai* is the reason the company refuses commissions against pilgrim or sovereign vessels; Angre's pass system is the model for the company's own passes.

**NSG changes it.** A Dutch post on Sainte-Marie coexisting with Baldridge is invented. The company is written as having opposed the slave dealing that caused the uprising, which is a moral retrofit and must be labelled as such in the backstory.

### 15. The Moluccas: Ambon, the clove monopoly and Banda

**Record.** In February 1605 Steven van der Hagen took the Portuguese fort at Ambon for the VOC without a shot, and local rulers granted clove contracts; Ambon was the VOC's Asian headquarters from 1610 until Batavia was founded in 1619. The company then enforced a clove monopoly, confining cultivation to Ambon and the Lease islands and destroying trees elsewhere on armed *hongi* expeditions. On 9 March 1623 the Ambon governor Herman van Speult had ten English East India Company men, about ten Japanese mercenaries (sources give nine or ten) and one Portuguese executed for an alleged plot: the "Amboyna massacre" that poisoned Anglo-Dutch relations for a century. In 1621 Jan Pieterszoon Coen conquered the Banda Islands with fifteen ships and 1,655 men for nutmeg and mace; about 2,800 Bandanese were killed and 1,700 enslaved, and the islands were parcelled out to Dutch *perkeniers*.

**Sources.** [Capture of Amboina (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Amboina); [Governorate of Ambon (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governorate_of_Ambon); [Amboyna massacre (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amboyna_massacre); [Dutch conquest of the Banda Islands (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_conquest_of_the_Banda_Islands).

**NSG uses it.** Ambon is the South East Asian chamber and the source of the spice cargo that makes the company's high-value, low-volume business model credible; the clove and nutmeg trade drives E4 (biodiversity) and S3 (affected communities) disclosures.

**NSG changes it.** The company is written as a licensed carrier for the VOC, not as its enforcer: it takes no part in *hongi* expeditions or the Banda conquest, and the 1623 affair is told as a warning about judicial violence. These are invented distances from a record the company shares by geography.

### 16. West Cork pirate ports: Baltimore, Leamcon, Crookhaven

**Record.** After James I made peace with Spain in 1604, unemployed English privateers set up in the remote harbours of West Cork. Sir Thomas Crooke founded a settlement at Baltimore in 1605 that doubled as a pirate market; Sir William Hull, a retired pirate and deputy vice-admiral, ran a depot at Leamcon; Crookhaven, Sherkin and Long Island were the other haunts. Around 1608 to 1614 the region hosted a pirate fleet, and Sir Henry Mainwaring, himself a pardoned pirate, wrote in his *Discourse of Pirates* (c. 1618) that "Ireland may well be called the nursery and storehouse of pirates". On 20 June 1631 Algerine corsairs under Murat Reis the Younger (Jan Janszoon of Haarlem), guided by a captured local fisherman, sacked Baltimore and carried off 107 people into slavery.

**Sources.** [Pirates of west Cork (Dictionary of Irish Biography)](https://www.dib.ie/blog/pirates-west-cork); [Sack of Baltimore (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_of_Baltimore); [Baltimore, Ireland, 20 June 1631 (Vallar)](http://www.cindyvallar.com/Baltimore.html).

**NSG uses it.** Baltimore is the UK and Ireland chamber, a Dutch victualling and prize-market tenancy in Crooke's settlement; Mainwaring's phrase opens its founding story. The 1631 sack, led by a Dutchman in Algerine service, is the lesson that the line between licensed and outlaw raider runs through the company's own country.

**NSG changes it.** A Dutch presence in Baltimore in 1621 is invented. The company has no part in the ransom trade in Barbary captives.

### 17. Flags and signalling

**Record.** Pirates used flags for intimidation and deception rather than identity. The earliest documented black flag with skull, crossed bones and hourglass was flown by Emanuel Wynn off the Cape Verde Islands in July 1700, per HMS *Poole*'s log. A plain red flag ("bloody flag") signalled no quarter; black offered quarter if the prize struck. Roberts flew several designs, including a figure standing on two skulls labelled ABH and AMH ("a Barbadian's head", "a Martinican's head"). Pirates habitually sailed under false colours and hoisted their own only at the moment of attack. Naval signalling used a few flags, guns and lanterns under written fighting instructions.

**Sources.** [Jolly Roger (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jolly_Roger); [Emanuel Wynn (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanuel_Wynn).

**NSG uses it.** The company flag, a black field with a company device, is hoisted only to signal a lawful stop-and-inspect; a plain white flag beside it means the inspecting party is unarmed. Guns, lanterns and a short flag code carry fleet signals.

**NSG changes it.** No false colours in the reporting period (a G1 commitment), and no red flag, since it means no quarter.

### 18. Crew composition: nations, women, escaped enslaved people

**Record.** Golden Age crews were multinational and multiracial. Kenneth Kinkor's tabulation of twenty-three crews from 1715 to 1726 found that roughly a quarter to a third of the estimated 5,000 pirates were of African descent, and in seven crews half or more were. When Roberts' crew was taken at Cape Lopez in February 1722, 272 men were captured, of whom 65 were Black and were sold into slavery; 52 of the rest were hanged. Escaped enslaved people had fought beside raiders since Drake: Diego, who fled Nombre de Dios in 1572, brokered Drake's alliance with the Cimarrons; Diego el Mulato, Havana-born, commanded in Dutch WIC service in the 1630s. Women were rare but real: Anne Bonny and Mary Read were tried for piracy at St Jago de la Vega (Spanish Town), Jamaica, on 28 November 1720, sentenced to death, and reprieved when both "pleaded their bellies"; Read died in prison in 1721. Most crews formally excluded women.

**Sources.** [Black Pirates (Vallar, on Kinkor)](http://www.cindyvallar.com/blackpirates.html); [Bartholomew Roberts (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartholomew_Roberts); [Diego el Mulato (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diego_el_Mulato); [Mary Read (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Read).

**NSG uses it.** The workforce register shows crews drawn from all five regions and mixed by origin, with the historical share of African-descended crew as the floor rather than a ceiling; Bonny and Read are the named precedents for women at sea in the company's own history.

**NSG changes it.** Women serve on equal terms and all roles are open (invented against the record of Roberts' article); the company has no "boys" aboard.

### 19. Pressed and forced men

**Record.** Navies recruited by impressment, and pirates by "forcing": skilled men such as surgeons, carpenters, coopers, navigators and musicians were routinely taken from prizes and made to serve, sometimes signing the articles under duress. Almost every surgeon known to have served with Golden Age pirates appears to have been forced. At trial the forced-man defence could acquit: Samuel Bellamy's carpenters Thomas Davis and Thomas South were freed at Boston in 1717 on that ground, and volunteers sometimes asked to be "forced" for show. Roberts' musicians were granted only the Sabbath off.

**Sources.** [The pirate surgeon (waynesavage.com)](https://waynesavage.com/the-pirate-surgeon/); [Pirate Surgeon's Journal p. 6](http://piratesurgeon.com/pages/surgeon_pages/surgeon_history6.htm); [Impressment (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impressment).

**NSG uses it.** Forced recruitment is written into the company's history as a real past practice, abolished by an amendment to the Articles, and reported under S1 as a remediated past impact with the amendment date in canon.

**NSG changes it.** Abolition itself is the change; refusal condition 6 (no pressing of captured crews) makes it a live rule.

### 20. Money: guilders of account, stuivers, rijksdaalder, piece of eight

**Record.** Dutch companies kept books in a money of account: the guilder of 20 stuivers, each of 16 penningen. Most silver in hand was the rijksdaalder (declared the national coin in 1606 at 47 stuivers, raised to 48 in 1608) or the Spanish real de a ocho, the piece of eight. Because silver fetched roughly a fifth more in Asia and coin drained east, the VOC in 1640 rated both the rijksdaalder and the piece of eight at 60 stuivers in Asia while they passed for about 50 at home: the "light money / heavy money" gap. In 1645 the VOC struck its first coin in the Indies, the Batavia crown, in denominations of 48, 24 and 12 stuivers, cut from Spanish silver.

**Sources.** [Dutch rijksdaalder (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_rijksdaalder); [Netherlands Indies guilder (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands_Indies_guilder); [Silver crown of 48 stuivers, 1645 (Rijksmuseum)](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/object/Zilveren-kroon-reaal-van-48-stuivers-eerste-in-Indonesie-geslagen-Nederlandse-munt--cd953808f6c444055caf6034d51ff468).

**NSG uses it.** EUR is the only currency in any reported figure. Guilders of 20 stuivers appear as a flavour column in prose tables; pieces of eight and rijksdaalders are the cash vocabulary of ships and bases; the Indian Ocean and Ambon chambers carry an "Asian agio" note; the 48-stuiver crown is the internal payslip unit.

**NSG changes it.** The notional EUR/guilder conversion used in flavour columns is invented and must be stated wherever used.

## Myths

- **Walking the plank.** No documented case in the Golden Age. The handful of recorded instances (for example the *Redpole*, 1822, and the Dutch brig *Vhan Fredericka*, 1829) are nineteenth-century. Fiction, not record. ([Walking the plank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walking_the_plank))
- **Buried treasure and treasure maps.** One documented burial: William Kidd left a chest and boxes of gold and silver on Gardiners Island in June 1699 with the owner's permission, and the goods were recovered for his trial. Pirates spent plunder; the X-marked map is Stevenson's. ([Buried treasure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buried_treasure))
- **Universal eyepatches and hook hands.** No primary-source evidence of eyepatches as pirate dress; wooden legs and prostheses existed because amputation was common, which is what the compensation tariffs are for. ([IFLScience on eyepatches](https://www.iflscience.com/you-probably-believe-the-myth-about-why-pirates-wore-eye-patches-70246))
- **Parrots on shoulders.** Sailors did carry exotic birds home as valuable cargo; the shoulder parrot is Long John Silver's Captain Flint (1883). ([Pirates in popular culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirates_in_the_arts_and_popular_culture))
- **The "pirate accent".** "Arrr" and the rest are Robert Newton's exaggerated West Country accent as Long John Silver in Disney's *Treasure Island* (1950). A Zeeland crew spoke Zeelandic Dutch; the company's English voice is a modern brand choice, and the style guide must say so. ([History.com on the pirate accent](https://www.history.com/articles/pirate-talk-accent-origins-robert-newton))
- **"Grog" and the rum ration as seventeenth-century staples.** Grog dates from 1740; the lemon ration from 1795. See practice 7.
- **The skull-and-crossbones as the universal pirate flag.** Black flags appear from about 1700; before that, national or false colours and red flags. See practice 17.
- **Libertalia, the pirate utopia on Madagascar.** Appears only in Johnson (1724); regarded as fiction. The real Sainte-Marie was a trading post that dealt in enslaved people. See practice 14.
- **Pirates as egalitarian heroes.** Shares and elections were real; so were slave dealing and torture. The lore keeps the first and repudiates the second explicitly.
- **Keelhauling as a pirate punishment.** A Dutch naval punishment; the pirate signature was marooning.

## Unverified

Claims the lore leans on that the record does not confirm, each tagged.

- **Size of Dutch privateer bonds in 1621.** Verified only for later wars: 20,000 guilders in the Second and Third Anglo-Dutch Wars and 30,000 in 1705 (Zeeuwse Ankers). No figure found for the 1620s. Any bond size the company states for its founding period is **invented**; the later figures may be quoted as the historical range.
- **The Admiralty's exact percentage of prizes.** Verified: the Stadholder as Admiral-General took a tenth of net proceeds; a 1574 Zeeland arrangement gave the prince a tenth and the province a fifth; English practice gave the Crown or Lord High Admiral a tenth to a fifth. Not verified: a fixed Admiralty college percentage in the 1600s, distinct from the Stadholder's tenth, court fees and *rantsoenpenningen*. The company's chamber fee percentage is **invented**, chosen to sit inside the tenth-to-fifth range.
- **Exquemelin's *chasse-partie* as Morgan's actual articles.** Exquemelin sailed with Morgan and describes the custom; that his text reproduces a specific signed document is **unverified**.
- **Number of Japanese mercenaries executed at Ambon in 1623.** Sources give nine or ten; **unverified** which is correct.
- **Banda population "reduced to about a thousand".** A widely repeated estimate; the killed and enslaved figures (2,800 and 1,700) are sourced, the pre-conquest total is **unverified**.
- **The seaman's payout after Matanzas (often quoted as seventeen months' wages).** Not confirmed in the sources consulted; **unverified**. Heyn's 7,000 guilders and the 50 per cent dividend are sourced.
- **Dutch presence at Baltimore, Port Royal and Sainte-Marie in the years stated.** No record of a Dutch chamber at any of them; all three tenancies are **invented**, as practices 13, 14 and 16 state.
- **Client-issued letters of marque.** Historically issued only by states or their governors; the company's model is **invented**.
- **Survival of a 1621 rederij as a modern N.V.** **Invented**; the backstory must explain it.
