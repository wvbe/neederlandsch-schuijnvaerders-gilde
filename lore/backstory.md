# The Backstory — four centuries o' sailin' aslant

## In the Council's voice

The Neederlandsch Schuijnvaerders Gilde was founded in 1621 at Vlissingen by Zeeland
skippers who held a commissie (a letter o' marque) an' a grudge. It be, today, a public
company under Dutch law with its seat still at Vlissingen an' its Prize Council still at
Middelburg, a fleet of 25 wooden ships, 5 chambers of operation on four oceans, an' about
2,300 souls under the articles. It has never owned a hull driven by anythin' but wind an'
muscle, an' by its own articles it never will. In the year 102024-092025 it drew up its
first reckonin' o' the year (sustainability statement) under the Brussels Articles (CSRD),
ahead o' the date the law demands, which for the Gilde falls in the financial years starting
on or after 1 January 2027 (FY2028 for NSG's October year). This be the story o' how a
company o' the 1600s came to file it.

## 1. The foundin', 1621

When the Twelve Years' Truce with Spain ran out on 9 April 1621, the privateers o'
Vlissingen — the *kapers*, who had lain idle since 1609 — put to sea again within the
month. Most sailed under the new West-Indische Compagnie, chartered that June. A handful
would not. They had watched the great Companies swallow the trade o' the Indies an' the
Atlantic, an' they meant to keep their own hulls, their own shares an' their own vote.

Seven skippers an' one Middelburg boekhouder, Maerten de Waeghe, drew up articles in the
back room o' a Vlissingen tavern an' registered them with the Zeeland Admiralty college as
a *gilde*: a guild, not a company, because a guild's members own it an' elect its
masters. They asked for a commissie against the Spaniard an' got one, on a bond De Waeghe
raised by pledgin' his house. The magistrate who read their petition called them
*schuijnvaerders* — slant-sailors, folk who sail aslant o' the law — an' meant it as an
insult. The seven took it as a name. They sail aslant still: neither Company nor navy,
licensed but never owned.

The founding skipper, Cornelis Adriaensz. Scheef, was elected the first Captain-General
by a show o' hands on the quay. He was voted out four years later for keepin' a prize
inventory from the hands, which be the first entry in the Gilde's book o' discipline an'
the reason the Quartermaster-General holds the ledger to this day.

## 2. The Founding Prize, 1628

On 8 September 1628 Piet Heyn took the Spanish silver fleet in Matanzas Bay, Cuba, with a
WIC fleet o' some thirty sail. One o' them was a hired Gilde jacht, the *Stormvoghel*,
with sixty Vlissingen hands aboard on shares. The Gilde's portion o' the eleven-an'-a-half
million guilders was small by the Company's measure an' vast by the Gilde's. With it the
Council bought its first retourschip an' — this be the part we tell the regulators — wrote
the **Matanzas schedule**: a fixed tariff paid to any hand hurt in the Gilde's service,
copied from the buccaneers' custom o' the Caribbean an' fixed in coin so no captain could
haggle it. That schedule, converted to euros an' raised many times, be the social
protection scheme we disclose under ESRS S1 today. Pirates invented workers' compensation
afore the landsmen had a word for it; the Gilde merely kept payin'.

## 3. Five chambers

The Gilde grew as the Companies grew, an' always in their shadow.

- **Kamer Vlissingen (1621).** The seat, the shipyard, the ropewalk. Where the articles be
  kept in an iron chest an' the Council meets under the Kermis moon.
- **Kamar Ambon (1623).** The Gilde was carryin' cloves out o' the Moluccas within two
  years o' its foundin', sailin' aslant o' the VOC's monopoly by the simple means o'
  payin' the Ambonese growers more than the Company did. The Company noticed. The Gilde
  kept a low warehouse an' a lower profile, an' be there yet.
- **Chamber of Baltimore (1631).** When Algerine corsairs sacked Baltimore in West Cork on
  20 June 1631 an' carried off the town, the Gilde bought the emptied quays for a song an'
  made them its Atlantic door, where the pirate fleets o' Leamcon an' Crookhaven had
  wintered a generation afore. Mainwaring called Ireland the nursery an' storehouse o'
  pirates. The Gilde called it a good careenin' hard.
- **Chamber of Port Royal (1660).** The buccaneer capital o' Jamaica, where Governor
  Modyford handed out commissions like ship's biscuit. The Gilde took commissions from
  whoever was at war with whoever held the cargo. When the Treaty o' Madrid closed that
  trade in 1670 the Gilde stayed on as honest carrier an' prize-auction house, an' when the
  earthquake took the town into the sea on 7 June 1692 the chamber rebuilt on the spit —
  smaller, an' with its ledgers in a chest that floats.
- **Chambre de Sainte-Marie (1691).** Adam Baldridge's watering place on the pirate round
  to the Red Sea, where a Gilde fluyt sold biscuit an' rope to every rogue on the Indian
  Ocean. The Gilde's own hands were on that island when the Malagasy rose in 1697 an' put
  an end to Baldridge's slave dealin'. What they saw there wrote the **Sainte-Marie
  clause** into the articles: no slave, no pressed hand, no boy aboard any Gilde hull,
  from that day to this.

## 4. The Vow of the Old Ways, 1700

Here be the hinge o' the story, an' the answer to the question every landsman asks.

By 1700 the Gilde had watched the century's novelties come an' go. It had watched the
great Companies borrow against the next fleet to buy the newest hulls, an' watched the
Nassau rabble spend every prize on rum. Its own hands had lost two fluyts to a captain who
trusted a new-fangled chart over his own lead line. At the Kermis Council o' 1700 the
Quartermaster-General o' the day, Grietje Pieters — a Vlissingen sailmaker's widow who had
kept the common chest for twenty years — put a motion that the hands carried by acclamation
an' that the Council has renewed at every Kermis since:

> *That what was true aboard a Dutch ship in the year 1650 shall be true aboard every hull
> o' this Gilde for ever: no gear, no practice an' no word not known to our founders. That
> the Quartermaster-General shall keep this Vow as she keeps the chest. That any hand who
> brings forbidden gear aboard shall see it go over the side an' forfeit a share.*

The reasons she gave be the reasons we give the regulators today:

1. **Every hand can master the whole ship.** A wooden hull under sail can be built,
   rigged, navigated an' mended by the people aboard her, with the timber, hemp, tar an'
   iron they carry. No hand o' the Gilde depends on a landsman's engineer.
2. **Our edge be skill, not gear.** Any rival can buy what we could buy. None can buy four
   centuries o' knowin' the trade winds by the smell o' the air.
3. **What cannot be bought cannot be taken.** A company that needs no fuel, no wire an' no
   spare part from anyone cannot be starved o' them.

The Vow be why the Gilde has no engine <!-- ooc -->(canon: operations.technology_rule)<!-- /ooc -->
in any hull, buys no power ashore, an' runs its chambers by daylight, candle an' whale oil.
It be also why a landsman's talkin'-glass, found in a hand's sea chest in the reportin'
year, went over the side off Ambon with due ceremony, an' the hand paid her share with a
grin. The Vow be a rule with teeth, not a costume; the Quartermaster-General counts the
kindlin'.

## 5. The long centuries

**1702 to 1713.** The War o' the Spanish Succession made Zeeland rich in prizes again an'
the Gilde with it. The Prize Council at Middelburg dates its present rules to these years:
papers examined, no breakin' bulk, adjudication afore sale, costs on the client when a
seizure fails.

**1782.** When Britain outlawed the ransom bill, the Gilde struck the hostage from its own
practice an' kept only the bond. A ransom be a promise on paper, never a person in the hold.

**The 1800s.** The smoke-ships came an' took the bulk trade, an' the Gilde let them have
it. What the smoke-ships would not carry — cargo no underwriter would touch, routes no
line would run, goods whose owners wanted no questions asked — the Gilde carried. It
shrank to nine hulls an' three chambers, an' did not die. Baltimore an' Sainte-Marie went
dark for a generation an' were relit.

**1929.** The Dutch state, tidyin' its company law, would no longer register a *gilde*. The
Council reincorporated the Gilde as a *naamloze vennootschap* under the law o' 1928 an'
wrote the articles o' 1621, the Matanzas schedule, the Sainte-Marie clause an' the Vow
into the statutes o' the new company word for word, so that no board could ever repeal
them without the vote o' all hands. The notary who drew the deed asked whether the
"no gear after 1650" clause was a jest. It was not. The N.V. exists so the Gilde may sign
a contract; the Gilde exists so the N.V. has somethin' worth signin' for.

**1950 to 2000.** The trade o' slow luxury found the Gilde. A spice house that wanted its
nutmeg to arrive with a provenance no landsman's line could forge; a collector who wanted
a cargo moved with no wire an' no manifest in any machine; island communities in the
Pacific who wanted a carrier that would anchor off a reef an' row the goods ashore. In
1974 a Rotterdam spice house, sick o' a rival's cornerin' o' the mace trade, asked whether
the Gilde still served a commissie. It did. The **Letters of Marque Division** was reborn
that year under the old Middelburg rules, an' has never once sailed without a bond.

## 6. Why we reckon

Three things brought the Gilde to the Brussels Articles.

First, size. By the Kermis o' 2024 the Gilde had 25 hulls, 5 chambers an' about 2,300
souls under the articles, an' a turnover that put it squarely among the large undertakings
o' the Union, above both marks the law sets. The law's clock for a company o' our shape
starts in 2027; the Council chose not to wait for it.

Second, a client. In 2024 the same Rotterdam spice house that revived the Letters of
Marque Division wrote to say that its own reckonin' required the smoke o' every carrier
in its chain (Scope 3), an' would the Gilde kindly state its own. The Boekhouder answered
in one line: *we have none to speak of, but we shall count it to the last stick o'
kindlin' an' show ye the tally.*

Third, an' truly first: the hands. The Quartermaster-General put it to the Kermis Council
o' 2024 that the Gilde had counted every plank, every hurt, every measure o' powder an'
every share for four hundred years, an' that the Brussels Articles asked for nothin' the
common chest did not already hold. *The landsmen have finally written down what the
articles always required*, she said. *Let us show 'em how it be done.* The motion carried
by acclamation.

So the Gilde reckons. The tallies be kept as they always were: in ink, per hull, in the
boekhouder's ledger an' the gunner's book, gathered to Middelburg by galjoot at the turn o'
each Kermis. Under the **landsmen's exception** in the articles, a notary's clerk at
Middelburg — a landsman, not a hand — conveys the ledgers into the Salacia platform, an'
the Gilde's own people touch none of it. The words in that platform be ours; the means o'
gettin' them there be a landsman's, an' we say so plainly.
